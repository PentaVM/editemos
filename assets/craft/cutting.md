# Cutting

A cut is a decision about attention. Every cut should answer: what does the viewer need to
see next, and when are they ready for it?

## What a cut must keep (in order of weight)

1. **Emotion.** Does the cut keep the feeling of the moment? A technically perfect cut that
   kills the laugh is wrong.
2. **Story.** Does it move the story on, or does it repeat something already understood?
3. **Rhythm.** Does it land at the moment the viewer is ready (the line ends, the action
   peaks, the music accents)?
4. **Eye trace.** Where is the viewer looking at the end of shot A? Put the subject of shot B
   near there, or the eye has to search.
5. **Screen direction and continuity.** Lowest priority: viewers forgive a mismatch they did
   not notice because the emotion carried them.

## Where to cut

- **On action.** Cut during a movement (a car turning, a hand reaching, a ball struck), not
  before or after it. The motion hides the cut.
- **On the look.** A person looks off screen: cut to what they see. The look is the question,
  the next shot the answer.
- **After the line, not on it.** Leave a breath after the last word: 300 to 500 ms. Cutting on
  the last syllable feels like the speaker was interrupted (unless that is the joke, see
  punchline cuts).
- **Before the reaction ends.** Hold a reaction until it peaks, then leave while it is still
  alive. Waiting for it to fade drains it.

## Kinds of cut

- **Hard cut.** The default. Most edits are 95% hard cuts.
- **J cut.** The sound of the next shot starts before its picture. Pulls the viewer forward;
  smooths a scene change.
- **L cut.** The sound of this shot continues over the next picture. Lets a line land on the
  listener's face.
- **Match cut.** Shape, motion or composition rhymes across the cut (a ball becomes a moon).
  Use rarely; it announces itself.
- **Jump cut.** Same framing, time removed. Honest in talking-head content; hide it with a
  punch-in (scale 106-115%) when the jump distracts.
- **Smash cut.** Abrupt change of energy or volume, usually for comedy or shock.
- **Punchline cut.** The cut itself is the joke: a line set up, then cut away mid-sentence or
  to the contradicting image. Only when the abruptness is the point.
- **Cutaway / insert.** A detail or context shot over continuous audio. Also hides edits in
  speech.

## Transitions

Dissolves mean time passing or a change of place; fades mean an ending. Never use a
transition to hide a bad cut. Between two pieces of the same continuous footage, use
nothing: a dip or flash there reads as a mistake.

Between unrelated moments in fast content (gaming, sport, creators), carry the motion across
the cut instead of stopping it: a **zoom-through** (the end of shot A punches in fast, shot B
starts zoomed and settles) or a **whip** (a fast lateral move with directional blur), each
about 6 frames, with motion blur only on those frames and a whoosh centred on the cut. Mark
impacts inside a shot with a short punch-in, a decaying shake and a one- or two-frame flash.
A dip to black between plays reads as a slideshow; keep it for documentary or calm edits,
where a dissolve or a sound bridge also tells the viewer "elsewhere".

## Pacing

- Measure it: median shot length and its spread. A short (`reference` analyse) of a fast
  creator sits around 1-2 s median; a documentary 4-8 s.
- Vary it. A metronome edit (every shot the same length) feels mechanical. Mix short and long,
  and slow down before the moment that matters so it hits harder.
- Cut out the dead air, not the breathing. Remove pauses inside a thought; keep the pause
  before a reveal.

## Checks before showing an edit

- The first two seconds hook: the best line or image, not the first one.
- Every segment starts before the action begins and ends after its outcome (set-up and
  payoff both on screen).
- No cut lands inside a word, no cut splits continuous footage without a reason.
- Watch it with the sound off once (does the picture tell it?) and with eyes closed once
  (does the sound?).
